A waqf (also written *wakaf* or *habous*) is an endowment in Islamic tradition. You set aside an asset — money, property, shares — and dedicate it permanently to a good cause. The asset itself is preserved; what it produces goes to the cause, year after year.

## A simple example

:::example ₦50,000,000 for orphan education in Kano
A family sets aside ₦50,000,000 to pay school fees for orphans. Each child's fees come to ₦200,000 a year.

**Given once:** the money pays for 250 children for one year. After that, it is gone.

**Endowed as a waqf:** the ₦50,000,000 stays in place and is put to work. Suppose it earns ₦4,000,000 in a year. That pays for 20 children, and the full ₦50,000,000 is still there next year to do it again.

After about 13 years, the waqf has helped more children in total than the one-off gift — and it keeps going, for as long as the waqf exists.

*These figures are only an illustration. Real returns vary from year to year and are never guaranteed.*
:::

## The three parts of a waqf

- **The founder** (*waqif*) — the person or institution that creates the waqf and chooses its purpose.
- **The asset and its purpose** — what is endowed, and which causes it serves.
- **The trustee** (*mutawalli*) — the party responsible for looking after the asset and making sure it is used as the founder intended.

## Why the trustee matters

A waqf is meant to last far longer than any one person's attention. That is why trusteeship is a serious role: someone has to keep honest records, make careful decisions, and answer for them. Read more in [what a Mutawalli does](/blog/what-a-mutawalli-does).

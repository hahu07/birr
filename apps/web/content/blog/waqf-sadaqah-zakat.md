Islam encourages several kinds of giving. They are easy to mix up, so here is how they differ.

| | Zakat | Sadaqah | Waqf |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is it required? | Yes, if you meet the conditions | No, it is voluntary | No, it is voluntary |
| How much? | A set calculation on eligible wealth | Any amount | Any amount |
| Who receives it? | Specific categories of recipients | Anyone in need | The causes you choose |
| What happens to the gift? | It is spent | It is usually spent | It is kept; its benefit is spent |
| How long does it last? | One payment, usually yearly | One gift | For as long as the waqf exists |

:::example One giver, three gifts
Fatima runs a small business in Kaduna.

- At the end of the year she pays her **zakat** to families who qualify for it. The money is spent on their needs.
- When a neighbour's child falls sick, she gives **sadaqah** towards the hospital bill. Again, the gift is spent on that need.
- She also sets aside ₦2,000,000 as a **waqf** for a community borehole. The money stays; the borehole's upkeep is paid for from what it produces, year after year.

Three different gifts, three different jobs. None replaces the others.
:::

## Which should you choose?

They are not alternatives; many givers do all three. If your goal is a lasting source of support for a cause, a waqf is the tool built for it — see [what a waqf is](/blog/what-is-a-waqf).

*Questions about whether a particular gift counts as zakat or sadaqah should go to a qualified scholar; this guide is general information only.*
